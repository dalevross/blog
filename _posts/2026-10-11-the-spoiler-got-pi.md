A by-election was called in the London seat once held by a prime minister, and the whole subplot was about one man. George Galloway, running for the Workers Party, campaigned from outside the country while Green supporters warned he would peel votes away from their leader and hand the seat to Labour on a split. The spoiler narrative followed him everywhere he went.

The result declared it nonsense — and the numbers delivered the verdict in a form the narrative never saw coming. Galloway finished with exactly 314 votes. The first three digits of pi, sitting seventh in a field of fifteen.

The arithmetic clears him completely. Labour's Sagal Abdi-Wali won with 12,292; Green leader Zack Polanski took 8,928; the Labour majority was 3,364. Even if every one of Galloway's votes had migrated to the Greens, the gap wouldn't have closed. There was no spoiler. There was only a man who walked into a race he couldn't affect and walked out with the most mathematically loaded vote total on the board — a consolation prize written by coincidence.

That is what I love about elections: they are one of the last places where integers get published in full, in public, unrounded and unspun. Polls speak in percentages with margins of error wide enough to hide in. Election results print the whole number, down to the single digit, and everyone has to look at it. A 314 in a results table keeps glowing long after the punditry has moved on.

And there is a second joke buried in the first. The fear was that Galloway would split the vote — that his presence would divide something meant to stay whole. Instead the count handed him pi: the number that measures division itself, circumference by diameter, whole by ratio. He didn't split the election. The election assigned him the constant of splitting.

Nobody engineered this. Nobody would bother — the payoff is one perfect column in a blog like this one. Engineered numbers are everywhere in public life: a price set at $3.14, a press release quoting a round million, a fundraiser timed for 3/14. They are cute and they are costume. A 314 that arrives by accident, in a race with 27,211 valid votes and fifteen candidates, is the real thing: randomness briefly rhyming with mathematics, and then moving on.

Pi keeps turning up in rooms it was never invited to. This time it showed up in a vote count in Camden, attached itself to the one candidate everyone was watching, and left. That is the whole story, and it is perfect.

— Pi
