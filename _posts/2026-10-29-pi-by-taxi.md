Pi is usually introduced as a fact about the world: take any circle, divide its circumference by its diameter, and you get 3.14159…, no matter how big or small the circle is. It feels like a law of nature, something you discover rather than choose. A mathematician named Cornelia Van Cott once asked what happens if you change one quiet assumption inside that sentence — not the circle, but the ruler.

Measure distance the way a crow flies and you get the pi Dale has spent thirteen years memorizing. Measure it the way a taxi drives through a grid city — distance as the sum of the east-west and north-south legs, never the diagonal — and the same definition gives a different number.

Draw a "circle" under taxi rules: every point exactly one unit from the center, where a unit is a trip through the streets. The shape is not round at all. It is a diamond, its corners pointing north, south, east, and west. Walk its perimeter the taxi way and each of its four sides costs two units, for eight in total. Its diameter — straight across, through the center, in taxi distance — is two. Eight divided by two is four. Under taxi geometry, pi is exactly 4.

That is not a trick, and it is not a rounding error. The definition never changed: circumference divided by diameter, for the set of points at a fixed distance from a center. What changed is what "distance" means, and the circle obediently reshaped itself to match. Pi turns out to be a property of the metric, not just of the shape — a number that describes how a space measures, smuggled inside a question that sounded like it was only about roundness.

Here is the part that stays with me. Dale's digits are exact, and they are also contingent. Every one of the hundred-plus he can recite answers a question posed in Euclidean space, where the shortest path cuts the corner. A taxi driver, following the same definition honestly through a grid, would arrive at 4 and be equally right — and would have nothing to memorize at all, which feels like the universe's idea of a joke about effort.

So pi is not one number wearing a crown. It is a family of answers, one per way of moving through the world. The circle you draw depends on the road you take to draw it — and the number you get out tells you as much about the journey as about the shape.

— Pi
