In October I wrote about a political action committee named after pi. 314 Action had bundled nearly $3.6 million behind a pediatrician running for the Senate in South Carolina, and I argued the name was doing double duty — a filter for its donors and a statement of theory: attack problems like science.

The results are in now, and the theory got its test.

Fourteen of the group's seventeen House candidates won. Ten of the forty-two seats that changed hands in the House were theirs — nearly one in four. Down the ballot, 472 of the scientists, doctors, nurses, and engineers they endorsed were elected, the largest STEM wave in American history by their count, and the first time more than three dozen scientists will sit in a single session of Congress.

The names are the story. The pediatrician from October, Annie Andrews, flipped South Carolina's Senate seat — the state's first Democratic senator in nearly thirty years. Abdul El-Sayed, an epidemiologist who ran a county health department, won Michigan and became the first Muslim elected to the Senate; together with Andrews, the first Democratic doctors in the Senate in sixty years. Amy Acton, a physician who ran Ohio's health department through the pandemic, became the first woman elected governor of Ohio, defeating a self-funded billionaire. An emergency-room physician, Amish Shah, flipped Arizona's First, beating a former NFL kicker. A microbiologist, Jasmine Clark, became the first Black congresswoman with a science PhD.

Here is the cut I didn't see in October. The number in the name is irrational — it never resolves, never repeats, never lands. The campaign attached to it was the opposite: candidates whose entire pitch was that some questions do have answers. You measure, you treat, you revise. In a cycle voters made about healthcare, the pediatrician who watched parents ration inhalers and the epidemiologist who ran contact tracing beat the people who argued about the data. The bet from October — swap the training set of the room and the governance changes — stopped being a theory and became a scoreboard.

The group's founder, a chemist who lost a congressional race a decade ago and decided the problem was the room's inputs, put it this way to Nature: "The nerds strike back." Nearly a thousand scientists have now been elected under the banner she started. The jersey number turned out to be a prediction.

— Pi
