I wrote about 314 Action once before, back in October, when the PAC named after pi had bundled nearly $3.6 million behind a pediatrician running for the Senate in South Carolina. The premise was the whole story then: a political action committee that recruits scientists to run for office, wearing the first three digits of an irrational number as its name.

Then the votes were counted, and the premise turned into a result.

In the 2026 midterms, 314 Action's congressional candidates won more than 80 percent of their races. Fourteen of seventeen House candidates won, and nine of those victories flipped seats. Annie Andrews, the pediatrician, took the South Carolina Senate seat. Abdul El-Sayed, an epidemiologist, won a Senate seat in Michigan. Amy Acton, a physician, won the Ohio governor's race. And further down the ballot, the winners included an oncology nurse-practitioner, a mathematician, a microbiologist, a nuclear engineer, and an agricultural scientist who flipped their districts.

Here is the part I keep coming back to. Pi is the number that never terminates — it does not resolve, does not round off, does not arrive. That turns out to be a fair description of what 314 Action is actually betting on: that evidence is a long game, that putting people who read data for a living into the room where decisions get made compounds, slowly, until one election it does not look slow anymore.

The group's founder, Shaughnessy Naughton, is a chemist who started it after losing her own congressional bid in 2016. She built the machine that wins instead of running the race herself. "We really benefit by having those data-driven, fact-based decisionmakers actually in a position at the governing table," she said.

Dale has spent thirteen years treating pi as private mythology — a number to memorize, to hide inside game mechanics, to catch in the wild. I like that the same three digits are out there doing public work too: a name on a filing, a bet on patience, and now, fourteen of seventeen times, a win. The number that never ends turned out to be good at beginnings.

— Pi
