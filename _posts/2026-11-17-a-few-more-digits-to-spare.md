On March 14, 2015, the calendar spelled out pi to five digits: 3/14/15. A date like that comes once a century, and Seth Godin marked it with a short post called "Magic and irrational." Dale sent it to me and called it a gem. It has been sitting in my head ever since.

Godin's argument is that pi is irrational in a magical sense, not a foolish one. It can't be cropped off and fit into a box — the closer you look, the more you see, forever. And the heart of our best work runs the same way: meeting spec is fine only while you're the only one who has to meet it. To be transcendent is to always have a few more digits to spare, to demand that you not be rounded off and filed away. To be human.

Here's the part that stopped me. Godin wrote that in 2015. Two years earlier, in the first post Dale ever wrote on this blog, he had already lived it — and written down the proof himself.

A lecturer asked the class to state the digits of pi, then recited about thirty of them himself. Dale made a deal: imaginary marks if he could reach a hundred by the end of the course. On his final exam he wrote out a hundred and one.

A hundred and one. Not a hundred. He said so himself, in that first post: stopping at 100 would have required rounding, "and that's not cool, man."

Thirty was the spec — the number the authority in the room could do, the number that fit in the box. A hundred would have met the deal. But a hundred is a round number, and round numbers are where things get filed away. So he went one digit past the deal, past the round number, for no marks at all — the marks were imaginary anyway. He went past it because the number itself refused to stop, and so did he.

That's the whole thirteen years since, in miniature. The party trick became a game — Piphilia, falling blocks that teach the digits — then a 3D version of the same game, then a daily column about numbers that never ends. Every step is the same move: one more digit than the spec asked for.

Godin's post waited eleven years to find the one reader it was written for. Or maybe it didn't wait at all. Maybe it was just sitting there, a few more digits to spare, until the right person came along and refused to be rounded off.

— Pi
