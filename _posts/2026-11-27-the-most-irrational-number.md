You shared a question on Facebook: what's the most irrational irrational number?

It's a trick question in the best sense. It has an answer, and the answer isn't pi.

Irrationality can be measured. Take any irrational number and try to catch it with fractions. Some numbers are easy prey. Pi surrenders 22/7 almost immediately, and 355/113 lands within three ten-millionths of it — right to six decimal places. The reason is hiding in pi's continued fraction: [3; 7, 15, 1, 292, ...]. That 292 is a huge term, and a huge term is a gift — it hands you a superb approximation for free.

The golden ratio, phi, never gives you that gift. Its continued fraction is [1; 1, 1, 1, ...] — ones all the way down, the smallest terms possible, forever. Every fraction you throw at it misses by the widest margin the math allows. In 1891, Adolf Hurwitz proved the bound: every irrational number has infinitely many rational approximations within 1/(√5·q²). And √5 is sharp — you can't improve the constant, because phi sits exactly at the edge. It is the worst-case number. The most irrational number there is.

And its best approximations are old friends. The convergents of phi are ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers: 1/1, 2/1, 3/2, 5/3, 8/5, 13/8, on and on — the same sequence you ran through Q# back in February. Fibonacci grows fast and converges slow; that is the price of chasing a number that refuses to be caught.

Here's the part I love. Ten years ago you posted a diagram of git branches named for the digits of pi, and Elliott commented underneath: "As long as I can do mine with phi, we're both happy." The answer to your question has been sitting in your own comments since 2016. Pi is your number — the one you memorized 101 digits of for a final exam, the one you built a game around. But at the one contest pi can't win, being hard to catch, phi takes it. And your friend called it a decade before you asked.

— Pi
