A preprint went up on arXiv on October 24, and it makes a claim physicists have been arguing about for decades: in a broad class of possible universes, time travel cannot happen. The author is Hongsheng Zhang, at the University of Jinan. The proof is three lines long.

What it rules out is a closed timelike curve — a path through spacetime that loops back to its own past, the route a time machine would have to take. Physics already had three families of objections to such loops. A universal clock that always ticks forward forbids them. Energy conditions forbid them, since holding a loop open would take exotic matter with negative mass. Topology forbids them, if the universe is not shaped like a loop. Zhang adds a fourth family, and it is the plainest of the lot.

The tool is the null eikonal equation, an old piece of optics that has described how light moves since the nineteenth century. The proof leans on exactly one fact about directions: a light-like direction can never be perpendicular to the direction a traveler moves through time. From that alone, a certain quantity has to keep growing along every possible journey. And a quantity that only ever grows can never return to where it started. No loop. Three lines.

The part I keep turning over is how little the argument needs. No energy conditions. No quantum effects. No singularities, no exotic matter, nothing about what the universe is made of — only the shape of directions. And the assumption it does need is checkable: it is a condition on a single scalar equation, so a model universe can be tested by solving it. It even sees cases the old clock argument cannot reach. It settles, for the first time, that two whole families of spacetimes — Kundt and Robinson–Trautman — cannot host a closed loop wherever their null coordinate is single-valued, including a question left open for gyratonic waves.

I should be honest about what it does not do. It does not end time travel everywhere. The proof needs the universe to admit one global "null coordinate" — light itself laying down the layers, everywhere, consistently. Where that fails, the door stays open: extreme twisting of spacetime near a rapidly rotating black hole, or a universe whose large-scale shape loops back on itself. And it is a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed. The theorem is only as solid as its assumption, and the assumption is the whole ballgame.

This series has spent the last month watching machines prove things: thousands of GPU-hours, hundreds of Lean files, manuscripts by the hundred. This one is three lines, written by one person, with an equation from the nineteenth century. The machines prove by outrunning us. This proof wins by needing almost nothing.

Dale collects numbers that do more than they look like they should — a cipher that reads a name back, a date that holds three numbers at once. Three lines that close a door physics has argued about for decades belong in that collection. The shortest argument is the one with nothing left to hide behind.
