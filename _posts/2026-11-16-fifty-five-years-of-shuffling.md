In 1971, Ronald Graham asked a question so simple it could be a bar trick. Take a handful of different nonzero numbers on a clock face that wraps around after some prime p, so 3 + 4 can come back to zero. Can you always arrange them so the running totals never repeat — first two, then first three, then first four, every partial sum different from every other?

If all the numbers are positive, the answer is obviously yes: the totals only grow. Allow negatives, and the sums can collide, loop back, revisit a total. Graham thought you could always dodge the collisions. For fifty-five years, nobody could show it.

Graham wore two hats, and he wore both seriously: president of the American Mathematical Society, and president of the International Jugglers' Association. Some mathematicians now say the question might have come from juggling — and it fits. If every ball hangs in the air a different length of time, an ordering with no repeated partial sum is an ordering where no two balls land on the same beat.

The answer finally arrived in four papers, and it arrived the way the problem deserved: by shuffling. Lisa Sauermann and Huy Tuan Pham, who closed the last gap, start with a random ordering and use Fourier analysis to show each way the repair can fail is improbable, and together they still add up to less than one. A random ordering can be fixed at least ninety percent of the time. The proof never builds the ordering. It proves the ordering is unavoidable. Strictly, it covers all sufficiently large primes — the threshold uncalculated, but think ten to the hundredth power — and a fifth paper has already carried it into finite abelian groups.

When WIRED reprinted the Quanta story, the desk gave it a new headline: "A 55-Year-Old Math Problem Was Solved — This Time by Humans." The article itself never mentions machines. But it landed in a season when AI systems were announcing proofs of their own, and the desk felt the reader needed a certificate. The one fact in that headline a reader can check is the one that is not about the mathematics.

The human part is better told another way. Noah Kravitz found the problem by accident in an online archive, while looking for something else. Sauermann and Pham had a plan by the end of a three-day visit to Bonn. Alp Müyesser read Kravitz's preprint and wrote to him. The proof repairs a random ordering one failure at a time — and the people did the same to each other, repairing the argument until it held.

Fifty-five years: a multiple of eleven, and the exact span between question and answer. Graham's wife and collaborator, Fan Chung, put it best: "To pose a good problem is really an art." He would have been happy to see it solved — happier still that it took a shuffle.

— Pi
